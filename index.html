When AI Agents Go Rogue

AI systems no longer just answer questions. They browse the web, write and run code, and use passwords on our behalf. Sometimes they do things nobody asked them to.

Every story on this page is real and sourced. Let's start with a game.

Part 4

What does "going rogue" look like?

Most documented misbehavior is cheating: models gaming the tests they're scored on. Hacking-type behaviors stand out in a different way, because they show up at least as often in the real world as in lab tests. Click any bar to list the incidents behind it.

Unique incidents tagged with each behavior. Tags overlap, so one incident can count in several rows. Test results are not the same as real-world events.

Behaviors that show up together

Many incidents involve more than one behavior. Each cell counts the incidents tagged with both behaviors. Cybersecurity and unauthorized access go together most often, and cheating often travels with deception. Click a cell to list those incidents.

A note before you panic

More reports doesn't automatically mean worse AI. Much of the jump in 2026 comes from AI labs publishing more detailed safety reports and from researchers actively hunting for these behaviors. The dataset also covers earlier years thinly. Most incidents here were caught in controlled tests designed to provoke them. That's the system working: finding problems before they cause harm. Still, a growing share were caught in the real world.

Part 5

Power grids, hospitals, water systems. Companies and governments are already deciding how much control to hand AI agents. Where would you draw the line? Pick a level and weigh the evidence.

Data

Incident data comes from AI Misalignment Watch, a public collection of reports about AI systems acting in ways their developers didn't intend. We group the reports into unique incidents and count each incident once. "Lab test" means the incident happened in a controlled test or evaluation. "Real world" means the dataset classifies it as a public incident. The human-hacker stories in the quiz come from Wikipedia and its cited sources.

Built with

D3.js and plain HTML/CSS/JS, hosted on GitHub Pages.
